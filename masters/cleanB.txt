A diesel engine is a type of internal combustion engine that operates on the principle of **compression ignition**: it ignites fuel using heat and pressure rather than a spark plug. Here's how it works in four main strokes:

1. **Intake:** The piston moves downward, drawing in only air (not a fuel-air mixture) through the intake valve.
2. **Compression:** The piston moves upward, compressing the air to a much higher ratio than in gasoline engines (typically 14:1 to 25:1). This drastically raises the air temperature to about 500–700°C (900–1,300°F).
3. **Power (Combustion):** Near the top of the compression stroke, diesel fuel is injected at very high pressure directly into the hot, compressed air. The fuel atomizes, mixes with the air, and ignites spontaneously due to the high temperature and pressure. The resulting rapid expansion of gases forces the piston downward, converting chemical energy into mechanical work.
4. **Exhaust:** The piston moves upward again, pushing the burned exhaust gases out through the exhaust valve.

**Key differences from gasoline engines:**
- **No spark plug:** Ignition relies solely on high compression and temperature.
- **Air-only intake:** Fuel is injected directly into the cylinder after compression.
- **Higher compression ratio:** Leads to better thermal efficiency and higher torque.
- **Fuel delivery:** Uses a high-pressure fuel pump and precision injectors to atomize fuel at the right moment.

Modern diesel engines often include turbochargers to force more air into the cylinders, improving power and efficiency. They also feature emissions control systems (like diesel particulate filters, EGR, and SCR) to manage nitrogen oxides and soot, which are byproducts of high-temperature combustion.

In short, diesel engines work by compressing air to ignite fuel spontaneously, making them highly efficient, durable, and well-suited for heavy-duty, long-haul, and industrial applications.
